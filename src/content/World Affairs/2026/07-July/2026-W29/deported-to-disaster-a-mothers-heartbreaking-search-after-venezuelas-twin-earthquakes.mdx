## The Cruel Irony of a Homecoming: Daniel Núñez's Final Journey

Oswadeliz Núñez once argued with her son, Daniel, about a tattoo he got at 24. Four years later, that very tattoo became the agonizing identifier for his crushed body after Venezuela was ravaged by twin earthquakes. It's a story that epitomizes the cruel irony of fate, bureaucracy, and the devastating power of nature.

Daniel's return to Venezuela, after being deported from the United States, was meant to be a reunion with his mother. Instead, it became a fatal encounter with a disaster that killed thousands. This isn't just a story about an earthquake; it's about the catastrophic intersection of immigration policy, government protocols, and the raw, human cost when systems fail amidst chaos.

## From Deportation Flight to Disaster Zone: The Last Call Home

The day the earth shook, Daniel Núñez was among 146 people – 120 men, 19 women, and seven children – on a deportation flight from Miami. Landing at Simón Bolívar International Airport in Venezuela at 10:22 a.m., he was part of the "Vuelta a la Patria" (Return to the Homeland) mission, touted by Venezuelan authorities as a dignified welcome.

Just hours after touching down, Daniel made his last call to his mother, Oswadeliz. He assured her he was safe, undergoing medical checks, and would spend the night at a hotel in Macuto, La Guaira, before embarking on the seven-hour journey home to El Tigre. "No, Mom, we're here with SEBIN officers," he relayed, painting a picture of order and security. That picture shattered just 40 minutes later.

The twin earthquakes struck, and the very hotel where Daniel and many other deportees were lodged crumbled. Unconfirmed reports suggest as many as 12 deportees might have perished alongside him. What was meant to be a structured return became an immediate, unforeseen catastrophe.

## A Mother's Desperate Search: Navigating the Rubble and Bureaucracy

As the nation reeled, with thousands dead and displaced, Oswadeliz Núñez plunged into a nightmare. With no helpful information from authorities, she traveled to La Guaira, transforming into a relentless detective. Alongside friends and family, she scoured hospitals, clinics, and morgues, often sleeping only a few hours at a time. "We'd go into hospitals with eight, nine, 10 floors, climb every floor and check room by room," she recounted.

After days of agonizing search, convinced her son was buried beneath the debris, she was finally directed to an emergency morgue set up in a port facility. What she found there was chaos – "Bodies were lying on the floor," she described. The moment of identification was both a relief and a new trauma. Daniel's face was unrecognizable, his bones exposed. It was the tattoo on his left arm, one she had initially disapproved of, that became the undeniable, heartbreaking confirmation.

## The Aftermath: Grief, Delays, and the Fight for Dignity

The ordeal didn't end with finding Daniel. Núñez faced a fresh wave of bureaucratic hurdles. Promised a free cremation and burial, she was told she might wait anywhere from 10 to 30 days. Unable to prolong her stay and expense in La Guaira, she paid a private crematorium $680 to expedite the process. "We couldn't afford to stay there any longer, spending more money and time," she explained, highlighting the financial strain compounded by immense grief.

Her return home, with Daniel's ashes instead of his living presence, was a stark, painful contrast to the reunion she had envisioned. Yet, she holds onto a bittersweet gratitude: "At least I have his ashes... There are people who still haven't found their loved ones."

## The Deportation Pipeline: Unanswered Questions About US and Venezuelan Protocols

Daniel's journey to this tragic end began in 2022 when he crossed the US-Mexico border seeking asylum. In May, ICE agents detained him while he was en route from his construction job. His mother claims he had already decided to return to Venezuela by year-end due to increasing "persecution of migrants."

According to Núñez, his detention stemmed from a failure to appear in court over a traffic violation (driving without a valid license) in 2024. Despite paying a fine, he allegedly never received a court notice, sent to a previous address. While court records show Daniel had no serious criminal history beyond traffic infractions, his pending asylum case kept him in ICE custody. Núñez recounts the "psychological pressure" he endured to self-deport, leading him to sign the papers. He was promised a return within five days, but it stretched to 15, tragically landing him in Venezuela on the very day the earthquakes hit.

The US Department of Homeland Security spokesperson confirmed the flight arrived "without incident" and stated, "once an individual is no longer in ICE custody, ICE is no longer responsible for that person." This stance, while legally precise, offers little comfort to grieving families like the Núñezes.

## Oswadeliz Núñez's Call for Justice and Transparency

Oswadeliz Núñez, a lawyer herself, is far from silenced. She uses social media to amplify her voice and plans to further her legal education to pursue justice. Her fight extends beyond her personal tragedy; it's a demand for systemic change.

She implores the Venezuelan government for greater transparency for grieving families and to implement more humane protocols for deportees, especially those without criminal records. Her powerful words resonate: "They are not sacks of potatoes. They are human beings. They are handing over human beings." For Núñez, her son's death is not just a natural disaster casualty; it's a testament to the failures of a system that must be held accountable. "All I ask God is that these deaths not go unpunished, because my son was not a criminal." The buzz around this story isn't just about a tragedy; it's about a mother's relentless quest for dignity and accountability in the face of unimaginable loss.
