The buzz is palpable. Germany's swimmers are heading to the European Championships in Paris (July 31 – August 16) with a renewed sense of hope, driven by the phenomenal talent of Olympic champion and world record holder Lukas Märtens. For many, this feels like a return to glory, a long-awaited resurgence for a nation that once dominated the pools. But for legendary swimmer Franziska van Almsick, the picture is far more complex – and alarmingly grim.

While she celebrates the individual successes, van Almsick warns against mistaking a few shining stars for a healthy system. Her true focus? A national emergency unfolding quietly, yet with devastating consequences: the dramatic decline of swimming education and infrastructure across Germany.

## Germany's Swim Hopes: A Mirage or a Resurgence at the European Championships?

With Lukas Märtens leading the charge, the German men's team is arguably its strongest in years. "Yes, definitely. And it was about time," van Almsick readily admits. "I'm thrilled we've reconnected, especially in the long-distance events where we've consistently shown our strength." It's a sentiment many fans share, eager to see Germany reclaim its place on the international swimming stage.

### Lukas Märtens and the Men's Team: A Beacon of Hope for German Swimming

Märtens, fresh off an Olympic gold and world record, has undoubtedly injected vital energy and visibility into German swimming. Van Almsick acknowledges his impact: "For swimming, this success was incredibly important. It showed that we haven't disappeared into obscurity." After years of struggling to make a significant impact post-dominance, Märtens' achievements signal a potential turning point.

### The Thinning Ranks: Where Are Germany's Future Female Swimmers?

Yet, van Almsick quickly pivots from optimism to a stark reality check. While women like Isabell Gose and Anna Elendt offer individual medal potential, the overall breadth of talent is concerning. "After that, it gets thin," she states bluntly. "Perhaps we made the mistake in Germany of celebrating one or two outstanding names so much that we immediately thought: German swimming is back. It's not that simple."

She recalls a time when German teams for European Championships numbered around 40 athletes, a mix of equally strong men and women. Today, the team size is similar, but the distribution of top talent is skewed. "If only one or two women have realistic medal chances, it shows that the base has become significantly narrower." This raises a critical question: is Germany building a sustainable swimming future, or merely celebrating fleeting individual brilliance?

## Beyond the Medals: Franziska van Almsick Unpacks Germany's Drowning Epidemic

For van Almsick, however, the state of elite competitive swimming, while important, pales in comparison to a far more pressing issue: the alarming inability of German children to learn how to swim safely. This isn't just about athletic potential; it's about survival. "Swimming saves lives," she emphasizes, highlighting the life-or-death stakes.

### The Shocking Reality of German Swim Education and Rising Drowning Rates

The statistics are chilling. In June alone, 99 people drowned in Germany, many of them young individuals who likely overestimated their abilities in the water. This isn't just an accident; van Almsick describes it as a "dramatic" situation, a direct consequence of systemic failure. Families face two-to-three-year waiting lists for basic swimming courses, while schools nationwide struggle to provide adequate swimming instruction. The very foundation of water safety is crumbling.

### Dilapidated Pools and Endless Waiting Lists: A National Shame for Germany

The root of the problem lies in Germany's decaying infrastructure. "Every second swimming pool is dilapidated, needs renovation, or is closing down entirely," van Almsick laments. The country is facing a severe shortage of functional swimming facilities. This isn't just an inconvenience for recreational swimmers; it's a direct impediment to life-saving education. "The swimming education in Germany is getting worse rather than better," she observes, her years of advocacy now tinged with deep sadness. For her, swimming isn't just a sport or a hobby – it's "lifesaving."

Her foundation focuses on elementary schools, recognizing that the ideal age for children to learn to swim and earn their "Seepferdchen" (seahorse badge) is between five and seven. "Every child should leave elementary school being able to swim safely," she asserts. Since all children are mandated to attend school, it's the perfect touchpoint to ensure universal swim education, regardless of parental background. Yet, Germany is failing to provide this crucial skill nationwide.

## If She Were Sports Minister: Van Almsick's Bold Reforms for German Swim Safety

Van Almsick believes the time for talk is over; decisive action is needed. When asked what she would do if she were Sports Minister, her agenda is clear and uncompromising:

### Making Swim Lessons Mandatory in German Schools

First and foremost, she would push for **nationwide mandatory swimming instruction** to be firmly integrated into educational curricula. "Children are of compulsory school age – then they should also have the right to learn to swim," she argues. This ensures that every child, irrespective of their socio-economic background, gains a fundamental life skill.

### Rebuilding Germany's Public Swimming Pool Infrastructure

Secondly, van Almsick advocates for **significantly stronger federal support for municipalities** in maintaining their swimming facilities. While she acknowledges that not every small town needs its own pool, a comprehensive analysis is needed to identify gaps and ensure nationwide coverage. "The federal government should co-finance the construction and operation of swimming pools more strongly," she states, recognizing that local governments simply cannot bear the substantial costs alone.

### A National Call to Action: Boosting Swim Awareness Across Germany

Finally, she would launch a **large, nationwide campaign for learning to swim**. This isn't just about policy; it's about shifting cultural awareness and emphasizing the critical importance of water safety for every German citizen.

## Corporate Responsibility: Businesses Stepping Up Where Politics Falters

Van Almsick also champions initiatives that show practical solutions beyond state action. She is the patron of "Poolschool," a program where companies like Desjoyaux Poolschool provide exhibition pools for children to learn to swim and earn their Seepferdchen for free.

### The "Poolschool" Initiative: A Model for Change in Swim Education?

"It shows that not only politics, but also companies can take responsibility," she explains. This initiative directly addresses the problem of inaccessible facilities, providing a practical, immediate solution. "For years we've been talking about swimming pools decaying or closing. At the same time, we wonder about rising drowning figures. Eventually, talking is no longer enough – then you have to act."

## Why This Buzz Matters: A Call to Action for Germany's Future

Franziska van Almsick’s interview cuts through the celebratory noise surrounding German swimming's competitive revival. While the nation cheers for Märtens' gold, she forces us to confront a far more tragic reality: a country where learning to swim, once a given, is becoming a luxury. This isn't just a sports story; it's a critical social issue demanding urgent attention. Germany's future, both in the pool and out of it, depends on how quickly and effectively it can address this brewing crisis. The time for action is now, before more lives are lost.
